Curriculum Vitae

I am a Ph.D. Candidate in Political Science, a Knight-Hennessy Scholar, and a Graduate Fellow at the Immigration Policy Lab at Stanford University.

I study the international politics of forced displacement. My research asks how host governments respond when war forces civilians across borders, and how donor governments and international institutions within the refugee regime shape those responses. This agenda bridges international security scholarship on the civilian consequences of war with international political economy research on how institutions, law, and foreign aid shape state behavior.

I combine novel cross-national datasets with micro-level evidence using surveys, experiments, archival research, and interviews. My research draws on fieldwork spanning four years across East Africa and Southeast Asia, building on earlier work with Syrian and Afghan refugees as an aid worker in Europe. You can read more about my work in this profile from the Stanford King Center on Global Development.

At Stanford, I am a recipient of the Ric Weiland Award for excellence in the School of Humanities and Sciences and the Centennial Teaching Assistant Award for outstanding teaching. I am very grateful to J-PAL/IPA Displaced Livelihoods Initiative, King Center on Global Development, Freeman Spogli Institute for International Studies, and Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research, among others, for supporting my research.

Before beginning my doctoral studies, I worked with refugee organizations in Greece and the UK and as a researcher at YouGov, conducting public opinion research for government and the public sector. I hold a B.A. (Double First Class) in Philosophy, Politics and Economics from the University of Oxford.

Book Project

Cooperation with the International Refugee Regime

Research on the international refugee regime has focused largely on failures of cooperation, particularly wealthy states’ reluctance to admit refugees. Yet in the Global South, where 71% of the world’s refugees are hosted, the regime has achieved a striking degree of cooperation and expansion. Created in 1951 for a narrow group displaced by World War II, it now supports more than 40 million refugees through a global system of international protection and assistance. This relative success remains understudied in international political economy.

The book asks why this system has endured and expanded despite conditions that make cooperation difficult. For host countries, refugees can impose economic, security, and political costs and often remain for decades, while enforcement of international refugee law is weak. For donor countries, the benefits of financing refugee protection are often distant and diffuse, and contributions to UNHCR remain largely voluntary. Why, then, have host and donor states sustained and expanded the refugee regime in the Global South?

I examine this question using new cross-national and historical data, micro-level evidence from refugee-hosting countries, and archival and interview evidence from donors and international organizations. I have constructed two original datasets from archival records: the Global Refugee Return Dataset, covering 293 origin-asylum dyads from 1980 to 2023, and a new dataset tracing UNHCR financing from the early years of the regime to the present. I combine these data with surveys, experiments, administrative data, interviews, and fieldwork conducted over four years across East Africa. I leverage the 2025 U.S. aid cuts as a natural experiment to test the book’s core argument and examine how the refugee regime changes as foreign aid contracts.

More broadly, the book brings an IPE perspective to the study of refugees, using the refugee regime to explain how international cooperation is built and sustained around global problems concentrated in poorer countries, and what happens to those arrangements as foreign aid and political support for international institutions decline. The book is in active development.

Publications

  1. Lisa Blaydes, James D. Fearon, and Mae MacDonald. 2025. " Understanding Intimate Partner Violence ." Annual Review of Political Science 28:351-374.

    Violence against women occurs at high rates in societies across the world. The most common form is intimate partner violence, abuse perpetrated against a spouse or intimate relationship partner. We present a household bargaining model that seeks to clarify causal mechanisms and to identify key pathways by which economic, political, legal, and cultural factors external to households influence domestic abuse rates, gender equity within relationships, and rates of relationship dissolution. We relate key parameters to factors that differ across societies and over time, including economic opportunities for women, laws that criminalize domestic abuse, and social norms associated with gender equality. We review research associated with these topics to establish what we know and do not know about violence against women in households. While much of this literature is outside of the field of political science, we highlight opportunities for political scientists to contribute to our understanding of how and why domestic violence persists in the world today.

Working Papers

  1. Peace or Push? Understanding Global Patterns of Refugee Return [Talk]

    Return is the main way refugee crises end, yet there have been few cross-national studies of when it actually occurs. A central expectation of the refugee regime is that refugees remain abroad while home is unsafe and return as security improves. Yet using a novel global dataset, I show that most returns occur during ongoing conflict, while many refugees remain abroad long after wars end. To explain these patterns, I argue that asylum countries and international organizations play major and underrecognized roles in shaping return, largely overlooked by existing micro-level research focused on refugees’ concerns about security at home. Return is substantially higher when refugees face exclusion policies or conflict in asylum countries, and when donor-funded UNHCR assistance lowers the costs of return. I introduce the Global Refugee Return Dataset, covering 293 origin--asylum dyads from 1980 to 2023, which documents returns systematically undercounted by UNHCR and provides new global data on \textit{de facto} refugee conditions and UNHCR financing.

  2. Aid Cuts and the Subnational Foundations of Refugee Cooperation [Talk]

    What happens to public support for international cooperation when the transfers that sustain it are withdrawn? I argue that cooperation with the refugee regime rests partly on a subnational bargain: host communities bear concentrated costs of asylum, which foreign aid can offset through jobs, services, and other benefits. These benefits sustain the local support for hosting that can underpin refugee cooperation, which aid withdrawal may erode. I test this argument using the 2025 aid cuts in Kenya. Drawing on two nationwide surveys, a high-frequency panel and vignette experiment near Kakuma refugee camp, and 52 interviews with refugee leaders, I show that support for refugee hosting declined in hosting regions relative to the rest of the country during the height of the cuts. Within the host community, declines were concentrated among households most exposed to the loss of aid-related jobs or services, while national support remained stable. The findings show that host-community support for refugees depends partly on foreign aid, raising concerns about the durability of international refugee cooperation as donor support recedes.

  3. Does Building Business Networks Strengthen Refugee Economic and Social Integration? (with Sigrid Weber, Adam Lichtenheld, Jessica Wolff, Alex Wendo, Annet Adong, Clare Clingain, David Musiime, Andrew Zeitlin, and Jens Hainmueller) [Report] [Policy Brief] [Website]

    Does expanding business networks foster the economic and social integration of refugees in cities in low- and middle-income countries? We provide unconditional business grants and we randomize 8,000 refugees and hosts from 10 nationalities into weekly networking groups in Nairobi and Kampala. The groups vary in their nationality composition and meeting setup to induce ‘strong’ or ‘weak’ and same or mixed nationality ties. We find three main results in this Randomized Controlled Trial. First, cash grants improve business performance, economic outcomes, and life satisfaction. A novel finding is that cash alone improved business networks: recipients’ networks grow organically and become more diverse. Second, the networking intervention increases business engagement, collaboration, and learning, but none of the networking groups produce systematic additional gains in economic, social or psychological outcomes on average. Third, neither cash grants nor networking groups improve social cohesion, suggesting that livelihood interventions alone are insufficient to strengthen refugee-host relations.

  4. Beyond Encampment? Understanding Attitudes Toward Refugee Integration (with Adam Lichtenheld)

    Most of the world’s refugees live in low- and middle-income countries, where debates center on integrating those already present rather than admitting new arrivals. Yet existing research focuses largely on admission in high-income countries. We provide one of the first nationwide assessments of attitudes toward integration in the Global South, drawing on a survey of 3,326 Kenyans fielded after a reform was passed but before implementation, with embedded experiments varying policy dimension and refugee group. Support for integration is high, but citizens are less supportive of allowing refugees to leave camps than granting economic inclusion or creating integrated settlements, and support varies significantly by refugee nationality. Opposition is driven primarily by fears of crime, terrorism, and the spread of conflict. Ethnic kinship and close contact predict support, while economic factors matter little. These findings suggest that integration is politically feasible, but insecurity associated with free movement remains the largest concern.

  5. Partisanship and Blame Attribution for Foreign Aid Cuts in the Global South (with Alexander Yeandle, Ryan Jablonski, and Brigitte Seim) [PAP]

    Does foreign aid retrenchment reduce U.S. soft power? Standard accounts imply that withdrawing aid should damage a donor’s reputation most in countries that depend heavily on its assistance. Yet following the 2025 U.S. aid cuts, attitudes toward the United States remained surprisingly resilient in some of the countries most dependent on U.S. assistance. We argue that this reflects misattribution: citizens often blame their own governments for the cuts, believing that corruption or misuse of aid prompted donors to withdraw. Using surveys and pre-registered experiments in Kenya and Malawi, we show that citizens divide responsibility between the United States and domestic governments. Attribution also varies systematically with partisanship. Incumbent supporters are more likely to blame the United States, while opposition supporters blame their own government. The reputational consequences of aid withdrawal therefore depend not simply on exposure, but on the domestic politics of blame.

  6. U.S. Foreign Policy Shocks and Attitudes Toward Great Powers

    How does U.S. foreign-policy retrenchment affect geopolitical competition in the Global South? A common assumption is that policies that damage perceptions of the United States create opportunities for China, leading citizens to substitute away from one great power toward the other. I test this expectation using multiple research designs that exploit distinct U.S. foreign-policy shocks. I analyze panel surveys in Kenya conducted around the 2025 U.S. aid cuts and an increase in gas prices associated with the Iran war, cross-national survey data fielded around U.S. tariff announcements, and a nationwide survey experiment on U.S. aid withdrawal. Across these settings, negative U.S. foreign-policy shocks reduce favorable attitudes toward the United States but do not increase support for China. Instead, attitudes toward China also deteriorate. The findings suggest that foreign-policy shocks can generate a broader backlash against major powers rather than geopolitical substitution, complicating accounts in which U.S. retrenchment straightforwardly translates into gains for China.
